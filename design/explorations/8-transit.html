Every network policy rollout I have seen goes the same way. Someone reads the docs, writes a default-deny policy, applies it to a namespace, and then spends the next hour working out why the ingress controller cannot reach anything. The policy was correct. The rollout was not.

Cilium gives you a way out of this that plain Kubernetes NetworkPolicy does not: you can run a policy in audit mode, watch what it would have dropped, and only then enforce it. That single capability is the difference between a calm afternoon and an incident channel.

Start in audit mode

Audit mode is set per endpoint, not per policy, which trips people up. You annotate the namespace and Cilium starts logging policy verdicts without acting on them. Traffic that would be denied is marked and allowed through.

Reading Hubble flows

With audit on, Hubble becomes the feedback loop. Filter for verdicts rather than pods, because the interesting traffic is the traffic you did not think about.

If an alert fires and you do not act on it, the alert is wrong, not your attention. The same is true of a policy verdict you decided to ignore.

Flip to default-deny

Once the audit log is quiet for a full cycle, remove the annotation. The policy is unchanged. Only enforcement changes, which means the blast radius of the flip is something you have already measured.

  • Enforce one namespace at a time, starting with the least critical.
  • Keep the audit annotation in version control so reverting is a one-line change.
  • Watch cilium_drop_count_total for ten minutes after each flip.

The DNS gotcha

Nine out of ten broken rollouts are DNS. A default-deny egress policy blocks port 53 to CoreDNS unless you explicitly allow it, and the failure mode is not a connection refused. It is a thirty-second timeout that looks like a slow application.
